COURSE PROJECT · FUNDAMENTALS OF ROBOTICS AND AUTOMATION

A Voice-Controlled Pan-Tilt Robot Head with an OLED Face, Built on the ESP32 and Cloud Speech Services

A desktop conversational robot that combines local motion control, speech detection and cloud-based voice interaction.

Dharshan S25BEC0551
Karan N25BEC0550
School of Electronics Engineering Course code: BAECE339 Academic year: 2026–2027
01 / OVERVIEW

Abstract

Talking to a robot is natural, but recognizing speech and understanding language normally need much more computing power than a small microcontroller offers. This project builds a desktop robot called Sydney that gets around the problem by splitting the work in two. An ESP32 board handles the physical side. It listens through an INMP441 microphone, decides when a person has started and stopped speaking, moves a two-servo pan-tilt neck, draws a face on a 0.96-inch OLED, and plays sound through a MAX98357 amplifier and a 4-ohm speaker.

The language side runs on Groq's cloud services, which turn the recorded speech into text with Whisper, write a short answer with a chat model, and convert that answer back into speech. Simple spoken commands such as “look left” or “nod” are recognized on the board and move the servos directly, so they cost no chat request. Memory was the hardest limit. With no external RAM, the recording is held to four seconds at 8 kHz and the code checks the heap before every upload. This report covers the design, the equations behind the signal processing and servo control, the faults found during testing, and the limits of the current version.

Keywords: voice interaction, ESP32, pan-tilt servo, speech recognition, human-robot interaction, I2S, embedded systems.
02 / BACKGROUND

1. Introduction

Voice assistants went from novelty to ordinary household equipment in about ten years. Phones, speakers and cars now take spoken requests, and libraries and schools have started to look at what they can do [11]. A robot is a different case. It has a body, so people expect it to behave like one: to turn toward them when they speak, to look where they point, and to answer in a way that fits the moment. Surveys of human-robot interaction have said for years that the quality of the interaction depends on both channels together, speech and movement [15], [16]. A robot that talks but never moves feels like a speaker. A robot that moves but cannot be spoken to feels like a toy.

Building robots that communicate socially is not a new idea. Fong et al. surveyed socially interactive robots and set out design questions that still apply, such as how much expression a robot needs and how it should read the people around it [12]. Breazeal's work on sociable robots showed that simple, readable expressions can carry a lot of meaning even when the hardware is modest [13]. Dautenhahn described the dimensions along which social intelligence in robots can be judged [14]. Eye behavior gets special attention, because where a robot looks tells people what it is attending to, and Ruhland et al. reviewed the research on gaze in virtual agents, social robots and human-computer interaction [17]. These papers shaped two choices in this project: a face drawn on a small screen, and eyes that glance in the direction the head turns.

Speech recognition is the first technical obstacle. Deep neural networks replaced Gaussian mixture acoustic models around 2012 [3], recurrent networks were applied directly to speech soon afterward [4], and attention-based sequence-to-sequence models later matched or beat conventional recognizers [5]. A review by Li covers how end-to-end approaches such as CTC, attention encoder-decoders and transducers developed [6]. Many recent models rest on the transformer architecture [2], and Whisper is a good example. It was trained on 680,000 hours of labeled audio collected from the web and works across accents, noise and languages without fine-tuning [1]. That robustness matters here, because the microphone is cheap and the room is never quiet.

Understanding and answering is the second obstacle. GPT-3 showed that a large language model can pick up a task from a few examples in the prompt [7], and training with human feedback made such models much better at doing what a user actually asks [8]. Smaller open models followed. The LLaMA paper reported that a 13-billion-parameter model could outperform GPT-3 on most benchmarks [9], and models in this range can now be reached through a simple web request. Language models have also started to appear inside robots. Ahn et al. used one to choose among a robot's real skills while checking what the robot was actually able to do [19]. This project takes a much simpler route. The model only writes a short spoken reply, and anything that physically moves the robot is decided by plain code on the board.

The last stage is speech output. Tacotron 2 paired a network that predicts a mel spectrogram with a neural vocoder and produced natural-sounding speech from text [10]. Neural text-to-speech has moved on since then, and the voice model used here is a newer system, but the principle of generating audio from text with a learned model is the same.

A practical voice robot also has to know when someone is talking. Sending audio to a recognizer all the time would waste bandwidth and, on a metered cloud service, money. Statistical voice activity detection, such as the likelihood-ratio method of Sohn et al. [18], is the classic answer. On a microcontroller with almost no spare memory, this project uses a much simpler rule based on short-term loudness, with a hold time so that pauses between words do not end the recording.

Most published work on these pieces assumes a laptop or a server. Fewer write-ups cover the awkward details of running the whole chain from a 38-pin ESP32 with roughly 190 KB of free heap: how much memory a TLS connection takes, where a clip is held while it uploads, and how to share the I2S hardware between a microphone and a speaker. The aim was a small, working, understandable robot head, not a new recognition method.

Project objectives

  1. Capture speech from an I2S microphone and detect its end automatically.
  2. Obtain a transcript and a spoken reply from cloud services over Wi-Fi.
  3. Recognize head commands locally and move a two-servo neck within safe limits.
  4. Show the robot's state on an OLED.
  5. Keep all of this reliable inside the memory of the ESP32.
03 / DESIGN AND IMPLEMENTATION

2. Proposed Methodology

2.1 System overview

The ESP32 sits in the middle and talks to everything else. Sound comes in from the microphone, is measured and recorded on the board, and goes over Wi-Fi to Groq as one audio file. The transcript that comes back is checked on the board for a movement command first. If it is one, the servos move and the cloud is not asked anything more. If it is not, the text goes to a chat model, the reply goes to a text-to-speech model, and the audio streams to the amplifier. The OLED shows what the robot is doing at each stage.

Microphone → ESP32 (level detection, recording) → Groq speech-to-text → command check → servos, or Groq chat → Groq speech → amplifier and speaker, with the OLED showing the state throughout.

The servos have their own regulated 5 V supply with the ground tied to the ESP32 ground. An SG90 can pull a sharp current spike when it starts to move, and on a USB-powered board that is enough to reset the processor. The two I2S ports are kept separate, port 0 for the microphone and port 1 for the amplifier, so recording and playback never share a clock. In both configurations the master-clock pin is set to “no change”. Without that, the driver may route a clock to GPIO 0, which the ESP32 uses to choose its boot mode.

2.2 Sound level measurement

The INMP441 delivers 24-bit samples inside 32-bit words at 16 kHz. Each raw word r is shifted right by 14 bits to bring it into 16-bit range, and a slow running average removes the DC offset the microphone adds:

x(n) = r(n) >> 14(1)
DC(n) = DC(n−1) + α [x(n) − DC(n−1)],   α = 1/512(2)
s(n) = x(n) − DC(n)(3)

Samples are handled in blocks of N = 512, which is 32 ms of audio. The loudness of a block is its root-mean-square value:

RMS = √[(1/N) Σ s(n)²],   n = 1 … N(4)

2.3 Detecting the start and end of speech

The rule uses two thresholds on purpose. Speech starts when the block RMS is at least 800 for four blocks in a row, about 128 ms. A rolling buffer of 4096 samples (256 ms) is always filling, so the first syllable, which usually comes before the threshold is crossed, is kept. Speech ends when the RMS stays below 450 for 1.2 s. A lower value for ending than for starting stops the recording from chopping itself in the quiet gaps between words:

Start: RMS ≥ 800 for 4 consecutive blocks; End: RMS < 450 for t ≥ 1.2 s(5)

Once the end is found, the silence at the tail is trimmed to 0.3 s. If fewer than 10 blocks (about 0.32 s) were above 450, the clip is treated as noise and thrown away, and no network request is made.

2.4 Fitting the clip into memory

The board has no PSRAM, so the clip has to fit in internal RAM next to a TLS connection. Before storing, the audio is reduced from 16 kHz to 8 kHz by averaging each pair of samples:

y(k) = [s(2k) + s(2k+1)] / 2(6)

The buffer for a maximum of T = 4 s is then:

B = f × T × 2 bytes = 8000 × 4 × 2 = 64,000 bytes(7)

The buffer is kept only if enough contiguous heap is left for the secure connection that follows. With M the largest free block after the allocation:

M ≥ 40,000 bytes(8)

If the test fails, the code tries shorter buffers, down to 2 s.

2.5 Cloud pipeline

The clip is wrapped in a 44-byte WAV header and sent to Groq's Whisper model as a multipart upload. The upload is streamed straight out of the clip buffer, so there is no second copy in RAM, and the buffer is freed as soon as the transcript arrives. The text then goes to a chat model with an instruction to answer in at most two short sentences of plain text. The code holds an ordered list of chat models and moves to the next one if the account has no access to the current one, remembering whichever works.

The reply goes to a text-to-speech model that returns a WAV file. The board reads the WAV header to learn the sample rate, sets the speaker clock to match, and plays the audio while it is still downloading, copying each mono sample to both channels. Long answers are split at sentence ends into pieces of at most 190 characters. One secure connection is reused for transcription, chat and speech, and it is closed when the conversation ends. The microphone is paused during playback so the robot does not hear itself.

2.6 Local command recognition

The transcript is converted to lowercase letters and single spaces. Let V = 1 if it contains a motion word (turn, look, move, tilt, head, face, go, rotate, glance, raise, lower, lift, center, centre or straight). A direction word w triggers an action only when V = 1:

action(w) = V ∧ (w ∈ {left, right, up, down, upward, downward, center, centre, straight, forward, front, ahead})(9)

The words nod, shake and around trigger their own motions without V. Commands run in the order spoken, so “look left then look up” does both. If at least one command ran, the transcript is not sent to the chat model.

2.7 Pan-tilt control

Each SG90 is driven with a 50 Hz signal from the ESP32's LEDC timer at 14-bit resolution. The pulse width for an angle θ and the matching duty value are:

t(θ) = 500 + (2400 − 500) × θ / 180 µs(10)
duty = t(θ) × (2¹⁴ − 1) / 20,000(11)

At θ = 90° this gives 1450 µs and a duty of 1187 out of 16383. A new target is always clamped to the limits in Table 2:

θnew = min(max(θ + s·Δ, θmin), θmax)(12)

Here Δ is the step and s is a sign constant, +1 or −1, that is flipped in the code if a servo turns the wrong way. The head moves one degree every 12 ms, about 83° per second, so a move of Δθ degrees takes:

T = Δθ × 12 ms(13)

Table 2. Servo limits and step sizes

AxisCenterMinimumMaximumStep per command
Pan (left/right)90°20°160°40°
Tilt (up/down)90°60°120°20°

Three compound motions are built from the same function. A nod tilts down 15° and up 10°, twice, and returns. A shake swings the pan 25° each way, twice. “Look around” sweeps to the right limit, then the left limit, then back to center with a 0.3 s pause at each end. After any move the robot says a short confirmation.

2.8 OLED face

The screen has a separate picture for each state: sleepy eyes with moving dots while waiting, a microphone with live sound bars while listening, a thought bubble while the cloud works, and a pair of eyes that glance left, right, up or down during a head command. While answering, the code looks for keywords in the question and then in the reply and picks one of eleven icons (sun, cloud, rain, moon, music, heart, clock, star, light bulb, sad face, question mark). If nothing matches, a smiley appears whose mouth opens when the mean absolute value of the audio being played is above 600, checked about every 150 ms. Everything is drawn with line, circle and rectangle commands, so no image files are stored. If the display is missing, the rest of the program still runs.

2.9 Main algorithm

Algorithm 1. Pseudo code of the main loop

  1. Input: microphone blocks, Wi-Fi, API key.
  2. Output: servo motion, OLED picture or spoken reply.
  3. Initialize OLED, Wi-Fi, both I2S ports, servos at center.
  4. Loop forever.
  5. Read a block of 512 samples and compute RMS (equation 4).
  6. Store the block in the pre-roll buffer.
  7. If RMS ≥ 800 for 4 blocks in a row, allocate the clip buffer (4 s, then shorter) and check the heap (equation 8).
  8. Copy the pre-roll, then record and halve the sample rate until RMS < 450 for 1.2 s or the buffer is full.
  9. If fewer than 10 voiced blocks, discard the clip and continue.
  10. Send the clip to speech-to-text, then free the buffer.
  11. If the transcript has no real words, continue.
  12. If a head command is found (equation 9), move the servos, confirm, and continue.
  13. Send the text to the chat model (try the next model if refused).
  14. Show an icon, send the reply to text-to-speech, play the stream.
  15. Close the network connection.
  16. Wait for 0.7 s of quiet and show the waiting screen.
  17. End loop.
04 / EVALUATION

3. Results and Discussion

3.1 Test setup

The robot was built as in Table 1 and programmed from the Arduino IDE with ESP32 core 3.x. Everything was watched through the Serial Monitor at 115200 baud, which prints the loudness readings, the transcript, the chat reply, the free heap and any error codes. The tests were done indoors over a Wi-Fi connection. The figures below come from those Serial logs. Where a number still has to be measured, the table cell is left empty in the report.

3.2 Sound levels and thresholds

The start threshold of 800 sits above everything seen outside speech, and the end threshold of 450 sits above the quiet floor and below most of the speech readings. In the logged run the recording was at 3.8 s when the second of silence passed, the end of speech was detected, and after trimming the tail the clip sent to Whisper was 3.2 s long. One weakness is visible in the table. A steady background level above 450, such as a fan, would stop the end of speech from ever being detected, and the recording would run until the buffer is full.

3.7 Discussion

The two thresholds work because speech in the tests sat well above the quiet floor, but they are fixed numbers. A fan, a television or a noisy corridor would raise the floor. A calibration step at start-up, or a floor that adapts over time, would be the obvious next improvement.

The four-second, 8 kHz recording is the clearest compromise. It lets the system fit in memory, but telephone-quality audio loses detail, so quiet or unclear speech is more likely to be misheard. A board with PSRAM would remove the limit.

The command parser is simple on purpose, and it has known weak spots. Because “look” counts as a motion word, a request like “look up the weather” tilts the head up and is never sent to the chat model. In the same way, “nod”, “shake” and “around” move the head even inside a longer question. A stricter rule that only accepts short sentences would fix this, at the cost of missing some natural phrasing.

From the robotics side, the neck is open-loop. A hobby servo gives no position feedback to the board, so the commanded angle is assumed, not measured, and that is why the limits are kept well inside the mechanical range. The connection to the cloud skips certificate checking to save memory, which is weaker security and would need to change before any real deployment. The robot also depends on Wi-Fi and a working account, and a free account has rate limits.

05 / FINAL REMARKS

4. Conclusion

This project put a conversational head on an ESP32 by letting the board do what it is good at and handing the rest to the cloud. The microphone, loudness-based speech detection, servo control and OLED face all run on the board, while speech recognition, language and voice come from Groq. Recognizing head commands locally means a spoken “look left” moves the neck with no chat request, and the memory checks keep uploads from failing on a board with about 190 KB of free heap.

The main weaknesses are the four-second, 8 kHz recording, the fixed noise thresholds, and a keyword parser that longer sentences can fool. Future work could add a wake word, adaptive thresholds, certificate checking, a board with more memory and position feedback on the servos. The most useful lesson from the build was that memory and power, more than software logic, decided most of the design.
